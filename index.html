Prehistory & Archaeology

8 facts
  • The earliest direct evidence of humans in Shetland is a rubbish heap — a midden exposed by coastal erosion in 2002 and dated to 4320–4030 BC, made almost entirely of oyster shells.Ch 2 — The Edge of the World
  • From Shetland's outermost isles, no land at all is visible to the west, north or east. For its first settlers this really was the end of the world — the Faroes, 200 miles on, wouldn't be settled for another five thousand years.Ch 2 — The Edge of the World
  • By 3000 BC Shetlanders were farming with domesticated animals and crops. The farmers weren't the first settlers — but farming triggered a rapid population boom.Ch 2 — The Edge of the World
  • The Lunnasting stone, a slate slab found in 1876, is engraved in ogham script and thought to name the Pictish king Nechtan Morbet (reigned 456–480) — hard evidence of Pictish culture reaching Shetland.Ch 2 — The Edge of the World
  • The St Ninian's Isle treasure — 28 decorated silver and silver-gilt objects, mostly Pictish — was found by a local schoolboy helping an excavation, hidden in a wooden box under a cross-marked slab beneath a chapel floor.Ch 2 — The Edge of the World
  • The medieval Historia Norwegiae claims Shetland's pre-Norse "Picts and priests" did marvels building towns morning and evening — "but at midday they entirely lost all their strength, and lurked, through fear, in underground houses."Ch 2 — The Edge of the World
  • The Romans never mentioned Shetland by name. Pytheas (c. 322–285 BC) called Orkney Britain's most northerly point, and Pomponius Mela in 43 AD counted seven "Haemodae" that may have been Shetland. Pre-60 AD Roman pottery found in Orkney hints at trade links.Ch 2 — The Edge of the World
  • The Lunna estate in the north-east Mainland shows evidence of Iron Age occupation; the Hunter family home there was built in 1663 on the site of a medieval house.Ch 9 — The Shetland Candidate

Vikings & Norse Heritage

17 facts
  • By 889 the Picts had vanished entirely from the record. Shetland's archivist Brian Smith rejects the polite theory that they were "overwhelmed": "I think they killed them." One historian calls it "as clear-cut a case of ethnic cleansing as can be found in the entirety of British History."Ch 3 — Vikings
  • Orkney and Shetland became a refuge for renegade Vikings after Norway was unified around 872 — prompting King Harald Finehair to sail over and subdue them. That's how the Earldom of Orkney began.Ch 3 — Vikings
  • Earl Einarr — an "ugly, one-eyed" bastard son whose father doubted him because his mother was a slave — is credited in the Orkneyinga Saga as the first man to "cut turf out the earth for firewood": possibly the earliest reference to peat cutting in the isles.Ch 3 — Vikings
  • Einarr avenged his father's murder by blood-eagling the son of the King of Norway — carving an eagle into his back, cutting the ribs from his spine and pulling his lungs out through his back.Ch 3 — Vikings
  • Christianity arrived by ultimatum in the 990s: King Olaf Tryggvason told Earl Sigurd to convert or "thou shalt die here at once, but I will fare with fire and flame over all the isles." Sigurd converted on the spot.Ch 3 — Vikings
  • Earl Magnus refused to fight in a 1098 raid on Anglesey and sang psalms instead. Branded a coward, he was later murdered by his cousin's cook with an axe blow to the head — then sanctified as Saint Magnus, after miracle stories spread of Shetlanders cured at his grave.Ch 3 — Vikings
  • Earl Paul built a beacon early-warning chain on Fair Isle to spot invasion fleets from Shetland. His rival's agent infiltrated the island posing as a shipwreck survivor, volunteered to mind the beacon, and kept soaking the firewood — when the fleet sailed, the alarm literally would not light.Ch 3 — Vikings
  • In 1194 King Sverre of Norway punished Earl Harald Maddadsson for suspected treachery by confiscating Shetland from the earldom. From then on, royal governors — "fouds" — ran Shetland directly.Ch 3 — Vikings
  • Harald Maddadsson became joint Earl at the age of five and reigned 65 years — the longest of the roughly thirty Norse Earls. He also ordered a bishop's tongue cut out and his eyes gouged, triggering a Scottish invasion.Ch 3 — Vikings
  • Shetland didn't even make the job title. Its rulers were styled "Earl of Orkney," and the Orkneyinga Saga treats Shetland as a backwater throughout.Ch 3 — Vikings
  • Up Helly Aa crowns a "Guizer Jarl" — though a real Norse jarl was never the top of the hierarchy. That was the King.Ch 3 — Vikings
  • Around 1300, Shetland was "a small country, a distant appendage of Norway, 550 square miles of rather poor land, with about 700 farms," governed by Thorvald Thoressøn, lord of Papa Stour.Ch 3a — Shetland & Norway
  • As late as 1602 — 133 years after Shetland passed to Scotland — the Shetland Lawthing was still consulting the old Norse lawbook in a murder case. The Privy Council abolished these "foreign laws" in 1611, and the Lawthing vanishes from the record after 1612.Ch 3a — Shetland & Norway
  • The last Shetland document written in the old Norse tongue dates to 1607 — the end of written Norn in official use.Ch 4 — Post Medieval Politics
  • Under Norse custom, crimes committed at night were reckoned more heinous than those by day — a complaint against Earl Robert Stewart specifically cited his pursuit of enemies by night as an affront to this tradition. And under the Norse "ranselmen" laws, refusing to allow a search of your house automatically made you guilty.Ch 4 — Post Medieval Politics
  • Scat — the land tax paid in butter, cloth and oil — was a survival of payments to the Norse jarls. As late as 1690 an Act of Parliament confirmed udal law, the Norse system of land tenure, still applied to small holdings in Orkney and Shetland.Ch 4 & 4a
  • Bressay Sound, the safe anchorage that made Lerwick possible, had sheltered Viking longships four hundred years before the town's 17th-century founding.Ch 5 — Lerwick, Burgh of Barony

Castles, Brochs & Ancient Monuments

8 facts
  • Staneydale "Temple," a Neolithic ruin with a 40-by-22-foot floor, was named by an archaeologist because it resembled Mediterranean temples — but it may actually have been a chieftain's house, or Shetland's earliest assembly hall.Ch 2 — The Edge of the World
  • Jarlshof at Sumburgh — "one of the most remarkable archaeological sites ever excavated in the British Isles" — spans Neolithic to Norse occupation: a smithy, wheelhouses, a broch and underground passages.Ch 2 — The Edge of the World
  • The broch is unique to Scotland — and the old "defensive fortress" theory has largely collapsed. Excavations turn up no weapons, and a broch under siege could simply be starved out.Ch 2 — The Edge of the World
  • Despite being among the best-preserved in Europe, Shetland's brochs went largely unnoticed by locals until the mid-19th century.Ch 1 — Introduction
  • When Robert Hunter expanded Lunna House in 1663, he built it using stone plundered from a nearby ruined broch. The house stayed in Hunter hands for over 230 years.Ch 9 — The Shetland Candidate
  • Earl Patrick Stewart had Scalloway Castle built by unpaid local labourers who were expected to bring their own food — refusers were tried for a crime. After his beheading, the castle served as a court, then a garrison for Cromwell's soldiers.Ch 5 — Lerwick, Burgh of Barony
  • Fort Charlotte was built during the Second Dutch War, burned by the Dutch in 1673, and burned again by French warships in 1702. A bankrupted Lerwick physician, Dr Arthur Edmondston, later lived in a small apartment inside it.Ch 5 & 6
  • After Saint Magnus's martyrdom, a church was built on Egilsay whose ruin still has a standing 15-metre tower; Earl Rögnvald later founded St Magnus Cathedral, which still dominates Kirkwall's skyline.Ch 3 — Vikings

Fishing & Aquaculture

12 facts
  • Before 1469, Shetland's fish trade was legally funnelled through Bergen — but Hanseatic merchants began bypassing Norway to trade with Shetland directly, to Bergen's fury.Ch 3a — Shetland & Norway
  • King James V wrote to the magistrates of Bremen complaining that German fishermen were threatening Shetland fishermen with violence.Ch 4 — Post Medieval Politics
  • Under Earl Patrick, fishing was forbidden on Sundays — with prosecutions — and banned from May to November in sheltered voes, to preserve the safer grounds for winter.Ch 4 — Post Medieval Politics
  • Laurence Bruce banned Fetlar fishermen from inshore fishing in winter, while the weather prevented deeper ventures — then fined them for breaking the ban.Ch 4 — Post Medieval Politics
  • In 1631 the Scottish Parliament established a 14-mile protected fishing zone around Shetland and Orkney — no foreign fishing inside it.Ch 4a — Parliament of Scotland
  • In 1661, Gideon Murray needed an Act of Parliament just to fish at Shetland — sailors from Hamburg and Lübeck had such a grip on local trade that Parliament had to give him priority over foreign vessels until his boats were full.Ch 4a — Parliament of Scotland
  • Lerwick's 1817 petition for burgh status noted the town was the official rendezvous of the British deep-sea herring fishers.Ch 5 — Lerwick, Burgh of Barony
  • The 1840s herring boom crashed within two years, bankrupting Hay & Ogilvy — one of Shetland's largest merchants — and abruptly halting a Lerwick site that had employed hundreds of men.Ch — The Long Johns
  • Arthur Anderson's Shetland Fishery Company (1837) brought skilled curers from the mainland to teach locals — and is credited with breaking the grip of fishing debt-slavery: "The young men had tasted of freedom and would never again consent to endure what their fathers had to put up with."Ch — Shetland's MP
  • Lerwick's "floating population" saved its police force in 1889: councillors told Parliament that 300–600 fishing boats arrived every summer, nearly doubling the town — and won Lerwick a named exemption in the Act, alongside only Renfrew.Ch — The County Council, 1886–1890
  • In 1890, a Montrose fishcurer campaigned for a Shetland council seat while living on the Scottish mainland — arguing his proximity to Edinburgh, where the Fisheries Board met, was actually an advantage. He lost with 20% of the vote.Ch — The 1890 Election
  • A key argument against Lerwick going dry in 1920 was the harbour trade — as one P&O captain warned, "the fishermen would go where they could get their glass of beer."Ch — Drink and Democracy

Shipwrecks, Smugglers & Sea Legends

10 facts
  • Complaints against Earl Robert Stewart in 1575 included piracy and conniving with pirates; in 1650, Janet Robertson petitioned Parliament after her husband was robbed by pirates when his ship ran aground in Shetland.Ch 4 & 4a
  • By 1737 officials were reporting rising smuggling in Lerwick — and by mid-century the Customs' House itself was entangled in the illicit trade. Black markets in timber and spirits grew into "virtually an industry."Ch 5 — Lerwick, Burgh of Barony
  • Shetland men sailing in the 1850s accepted they might never return, habitually writing "if the Lord spares me."Ch 5 — Lerwick, Burgh of Barony
  • The tide race off Sumburgh Head was so feared that an Orkney sheriff wrote in 1822 that Shetlanders would not venture through Sumburgh Roost to vote at Kirkwall in winter — "the privilege even now would not be worth purchasing at such a price."Ch 7 — Votes for Shetland
  • A pseudonymous Shetlander calculated that a voter from Unst would travel 1,484 miles to take part in an election at Kirkwall — and that a full election writ's round trip via Leith covered 3,560 miles.Ch 7 — Votes for Shetland
  • The 1832–33 election result hung on a steamer: the vessel was so battered coming up the Minch that it limped off for repairs, and the replacement carrying Shetland's poll books reached Kirkwall days late — while 3,000 Orcadians waited in the town centre expecting the wrong winner to be declared.Ch 8 — The Isles Vote
  • The Don Juan, built for £40,000 as the UK's largest steam-powered vessel, ran aground in thick fog with P&O founder Arthur Anderson aboard. He got the mail off onto a small fishing boat; the ship was a total wreck — but he forgave the captain, who went on to command another company steamer.Ch — Shetland's MP
  • Anderson's first steam yacht was sold off to run blockades in the American Civil War; another of his yachts was presented to the Sultan of Zanzibar.Ch — Shetland's MP
  • During Lerwick's dry years, shebeen raids were foiled by shops with hidden buttons ringing bells in the back rooms, and paid lookouts signalling approaching police. "It was such a stupid law. It was broken left right and centre."Ch — Drink and Democracy
  • In 1925, a drunken man's fall into Lerwick harbour led police straight back to a basement drinking den — a £35 fine for its keeper.Ch — Drink and Democracy

Shetland at War

8 facts
  • A gang of Scalloway nobles razed the entire hamlet of Lerwick in 1614, and the Sheriff Principal ordered it "utterly demolished" again in 1625 — calling its people "godless and profane." It burned twice more: by the Dutch in 1673 and French warships in 1702.Ch 5 — Lerwick, Burgh of Barony
  • The Battle of Summerdale (1529) was the last pitched battle on Orcadian soil — Caithness Sinclairs against Orkney Sinclairs, with reportedly only one Orcadian casualty. The animosity between Orkney and Caithness persisted for over three centuries.Ch 4 — Post Medieval Politics
  • A 1650 act preparing Scotland against English invasion listed Orkney and Shetland's available forces as just 54 horse and 750 men.Ch 4a — Parliament of Scotland
  • Press gangs "hunted with remorseless perseverance": the sighting of an impress vessel sent Shetland's men into hiding, and those who ignored the alarm were taken at all hours — including on their wedding day.Ch 5 — Lerwick, Burgh of Barony
  • Arthur Anderson — later Shetland's first native-born MP — was nearly press-ganged as a teenager, saved only when his employer told the officers the boy was already committed to the navy. He left on a man-of-war in 1808, aged 16, with the parting advice "Dö weel an' persevere."Ch — Shetland's MP
  • One eighth of Shetland's entire population joined the military effort during the Napoleonic wars, according to a 1832 petition to the Prime Minister.Ch 6 — A Divisive Leader
  • Shetland's WWI death rate was one man in 38 — far worse than the UK average of one in 60 — with nearly 700 dead. The Shetland News sent 36 people across the isles to record the fallen, making Shetland's Roll of Honour one of the most complete in the country.Ch — Drink and Democracy
  • WWII overrode Lerwick's prohibition: authorised service canteens in the dry town were permitted to serve alcohol to the thousands of mobilised servicemen — partly to stop a black market flourishing.Ch — Drink and Democracy

Murder, Mystery & Local Legends

16 facts
  • Sigurd the Mighty, first Earl of Orkney, was killed by a severed head. Having cheated a Pictish rival — bringing 80 men to an agreed 40-a-side fight — he strapped his enemy's head to his saddle. The dead man's protruding tooth grazed his calf, the wound turned septic, and he died.Ch 3 — Vikings
  • Earl Harald "smooth-tongue" died in 1130 from a poisoned shirt his own mother and aunt had made to kill his brother. Jealous that they never made him such fine clothes, he pulled it on over their protests.Ch 3 — Vikings
  • Rögnvald Brusason was killed in 1046 while attempting a surprise attack — the barking of his lapdog gave him away. A century later, Earl Erlend died in a surprise attack because his men couldn't wake him: too drunk after feasting.Ch 3 — Vikings
  • Earl Jon's death in 1231 has two competing legends — lost at sea in a storm, or burned out of his house and stabbed in the cellar with "nine wounds." Either way, it ended a ten-generation dynasty.Ch 3 — Vikings
  • Bishop William the Old went blind after denouncing the "heresy" of the Magnus miracle tales — then had his sight restored praying at Magnus's grave, and promptly canonised him.Ch 3 — Vikings
  • In February 1577, around 700 Shetlanders gathered at the Law Ting Holm to testify against the oppressor Laurence Bruce — a mass democratic protest extraordinary for its era. One of Bruce's men had evicted a Bressay family whose mother had given birth eight days earlier, then spent nine days eating their entire winter supplies.Ch 4 — Post Medieval Politics
  • Earl Robert Stewart carved a Latin inscription on his palace wall whose botched grammar read as a claim to be King of Scots — provoking the actual King into a campaign against him. His son Patrick became Master of Orkney only after the mysterious death of his brother, and was beheaded in Edinburgh in 1615.Ch 4 & 5
  • James Sinclair of Brecks — victor of Summerdale and rehabilitated governor of the islands — died mysteriously by suicide in 1539.Ch 4 — Post Medieval Politics
  • Dr Arthur Edmondston's mannerisms are said to have inspired Claud Halcro, the grandiloquent bard in Walter Scott's The Pirate — Scott toured Shetland in 1814 with Edmondston as chaperone. A sceptical relative later warned that in family traditions "the geese are apt to develop into swans."Ch 6 — A Divisive Leader
  • In his final years the bankrupted Dr Edmondston developed "a particular affinity with striking members of the public with his cane." Tried for it in February 1841, he was acquitted — and died exactly a week later, after two glasses of madeira.Ch 6 — A Divisive Leader
  • After Robert Hunter of Lunna died in 1833, placards appeared around Lerwick alleging his "supposed murder (by poison)." His physician had been ingratiating himself with Mrs Hunter — who later bore the doctor's child out of wedlock, reviving the rumours. The family declined to investigate.Ch 9 — The Shetland Candidate
  • Kirkwall descended into mob rule in January 1833: over a hundred men and women, led by a piper and drummer and armed with clubs, three times attacked the Sheriff as he tried to declare an 11-vote election victory — tearing the clothes off the defeated ex-MP. One local died a month later of his injuries; his assailant was never tried.Ch 8 — The Isles Vote
  • Robert Hunter Bell — grandson of Shetland's first parliamentary candidate — lost the Lunna estate to debt, was divorced for desertion, drifted homeless between friends' houses, and died in 1907 of a gunshot wound at Symbister House, Whalsay.Ch — The 1890 Election
  • Bell's great-aunt left her fortune to his young siblings on a poison-pill condition: have nothing to do with Robert and his wife, or the money goes to a church in Edinburgh.Ch — The 1890 Election
  • On 12 November 1874 — during Lerwick's bitter deadlock when rival elections held the same day produced two competing town councils — the house of the disputed Junior Baillie was tarred.Ch — Two Town Councils
  • Captain George Kingsell, brought to Lerwick in 1923 to argue against prohibition, proved a poor advocate for the drinks trade: eight years later, in an alcohol-fuelled rage, he shot his wife dead before killing himself. Meanwhile a 1920s Provost claimed some 40 known shebeens operated in Lerwick — when the Chief Constable asked them all to be more discreet, everyone agreed except one: "and he's a cooncillor!"Ch — Drink and Democracy

Wildlife & Natural Wonders

3 facts
  • When Orkney and Shetland were granted to the Earl of Morton in 1707, the Crown pointedly reserved its rights to any hawks on the lands — the Earl got the islands, but the Queen kept the royal birds.Ch 4a — Parliament of Scotland
  • The dramatic cliffs of Noss served as the landmark for ships approaching the Bressay Sound anchorage.Ch 5 — Lerwick, Burgh of Barony
  • The Hoswick Whale Case of 1888–89 saw Sandwick locals successfully sue their own laird at Scotland's highest civil court over the rights to captured whales — and in 1890 the whale-case leader crushed the laird's son 106 votes to 16 for the local council seat.Ch — The 1890 Election

Plants & Rare Species

1 fact
  • Thomas Edmondston (1825–1846) was a Shetland botanist who corresponded regularly with Charles Darwin — his burgeoning career cut short at 21, when he was accidentally shot while disembarking a boat in Peru.Ch 6 — A Divisive Leader

Crofting, Knitting & Island Life

18 facts
  • In 1619 the Scots Privy Council tried to ban "rooing" — the Shetland practice of plucking wool from sheep by hand rather than shearing — but after investigating, reversed its own decree.Ch 4 — Post Medieval Politics
  • From 1602 you needed a licence to leave Shetland: shipmasters were ordered not to carry anyone without a permit from Earl Patrick or his deputies.Ch 4 — Post Medieval Politics
  • Fines under Earl Patrick were so harsh that one man who stole a single ling had to sell land to pay the penalty. Laurence Bruce once confiscated a piece of driftwood from Fetlar crofters — then fined them for complaining about the theft.Ch 4 — Post Medieval Politics
  • Scalloway's lairds locked islanders into debt bondage, forcing workers to buy supplies from the laird's own shop. Arthur Anderson wrote that crofters lived in "a state bordering on absolute slavery," forced to sell their catch for less than a third of its value — and vowed "uncompromising warfare with all who uphold them."Ch 5 & Shetland's MP
  • In 1837 Arthur Anderson suggested Shetland's knitters send knitted gloves to the new Queen Victoria. She replied requesting a dozen pairs of stockings — a royal boost for the hosiery industry.Ch — Shetland's MP
  • Walter Scott, visiting in 1814, described Lerwick as "a poor-looking place... The streets full of drunken riotous sailors, from the whale-vessels."Ch 5 — Lerwick, Burgh of Barony
  • In 1663 Parliament passed an act allowing the impoverished inhabitants of Shetland to import foreign drinking beer — for one year.Ch 4a — Parliament of Scotland
  • Before 1832, an Unst freeholder wanting to vote had to cross Unst, sail to Yell, cross to the Mainland, walk 20–30 miles to Lerwick, sail to Leith near Edinburgh, travel overland to John O'Groats, cross the Pentland Firth, and hike to Kirkwall.Ch 7 — Votes for Shetland
  • In the 1713 general election for Orkney and Shetland, a grand total of 14 votes were cast. By 1817, only 34 of the islands' 46,000+ inhabitants could vote — every one of them an Orcadian. Shetlanders had no vote at all until 1832.Ch 7 — Votes for Shetland
  • The earliest known Shetland vote: James Mowat of Ollaberry, on 5 December 1660, for a Commissioner to the ancient Parliament of Scotland.Ch 7 — Votes for Shetland
  • Dr Edmondston once unleashed a tirade at Arthur Anderson for daring to write "Shetland" instead of "Zetland" in a letter.Ch 7 — Votes for Shetland
  • Women could vote in Shetland's first County Council election in February 1890 — decades before they could vote for Parliament. Turnout ranged from 77% in Unst North to 12% in Sandsting and Aithsting North.Ch — The 1890 Election
  • One 1890 council win was celebrated island-style: a large fire on the hill above the victor's home, two more blazing over the defeated laird's lodge, and a torchlit procession to the winner's garden gate.Ch — The 1890 Election
  • A Bressay farm manager was kept on by the Marquis of Londonderry to establish a stud of Shetland ponies — destined for the Marquis's Durham coal mines.Ch — The 1890 Election
  • Lerwick voted itself dry in 1920 (945 to 432) and stayed that way for 26 years — the nearest legal pint was 20 miles away in Voe. When the Thule Bar reopened at 11am on 29 May 1947, first in the queue was a two-war veteran raising his glass: "here's to the first pint for 26 years!" A class loophole survived throughout: wholesale alcohol stayed legal, minimum purchase two gallons of spirits or six dozen beers.Ch — Drink and Democracy
  • Lerwick's 1930s "water war": when a new filtration plant turned the town's peat-stained brown tap water clear in 1931, the Ratepayers Association fought for years to bring the brown water back — winning a plebiscite 1,743 to 410 and briefly restoring it in 1935, before a compromise "strippit water" (clear, with a little peat) ended the saga. Charlotte Nicol became Shetland's first elected woman in 1933 on the pro-brown-water ticket.Ch — Paint The Town Brown
  • Before the 1860s, Lerwick drew its water from wells that ran dry when the fishing season nearly doubled the town's population — some residents rowed across to Bressay to scavenge water.Ch — Paint The Town Brown
  • The Lodberrie — built in 1772 and virtually unchanged since — is now famous as Jimmy Perez's house in the BBC drama Shetland. And the town's tight-knit elite were so interconnected that an unbroken family line links Lerwick's first council leader, Shetland's first native-born MP, and its first female county councillor — with a tenuous but genuine genealogical connection to John F. Kennedy.Ch — The Long Johns

Industry, Innovation & Space

10 facts
  • Arthur Anderson rose from Shetland "beach boy" — scrubbing fish and spreading them on rocks to dry, aged 10 to 12 — to co-founder of P&O. Twenty-eight years after its 1840 founding, the company had 53 steamships, 9 steam tugs and a value of £3.8 million.Ch — Shetland's MP
  • Anderson's Peninsular Steam Company won the 1837 government mail contract and cut Falmouth-to-Gibraltar delivery from three weeks to five days. He was advocating the construction of the Suez Canal as early as 1843 — 26 years before it opened.Ch — Shetland's MP
  • Anderson founded Shetland's first newspaper, the Shetland Advertiser, in 1836 — printed in Fleet Street, London, and shipped north. His agitation is also credited for the lighthouses at Bressay, Skerries and Unst.Ch — Shetland's MP
  • During his 1847 election campaign, Anderson chartered a steam yacht to tour the isles — for some outlying communities, their first ever sight of a steam-powered vessel.Ch — Shetland's MP
  • John Morrison built Lerwick's main pier, only for it to be declared unlawful; the legal battle drained him "not only of his money but of his will to live," and he died in 1821. His pier stayed in use for decades — renamed Victoria's Pier in 1845.Ch 6 & The Long Johns
  • Charles Edmondston left Shetland at 17 for the Carolinas, made a fortune in fisheries and wharfage, and built an opulent 1820 Charleston mansion that survived the American Civil War and numerous hurricanes. It's now a museum.Ch 6 — A Divisive Leader
  • Gas lighting reached Lerwick in 1858 — 51 years after London. The very first of the town's 25 lamps to be lit was the one in councillor John Robertson's shop; he was a founder of the gas company.Ch — The Long Johns
  • The Reverend James Campbell of Fetlar, co-opted to the first County Council in 1890, was a skilled amateur mechanic who built his own microscope — and invented a fine-adjustment mechanism for it that bears his name.Ch — The 1890 Election
  • The Udal League, founded in 1886 to push Home Rule and Norse-style land ownership for the isles, drew press coverage as far away as Hamburg — and after folding was reborn as the Viking Club, which became the Viking Society for Northern Research in 1912.Ch — The County Council, 1886–1890
  • Lerwick's 1931 water filtration plant — the one that started the brown-water war — cost £11,200 and was built by 40 unemployed men hired by the council, modelled on works a delegation inspected in Buckie, Manchester and Stockport.Ch — Paint The Town Brown
